# JUSTIFICATION — the design of the harness, and why

## 1. What the system may do

This prototype is for **scholarship screening only**, not a general people-ranking tool. It may assess synthetic applications using program, GPA, entrance-exam result, household-income bracket and achievements; it can return `shortlist` or `reject` as a preliminary screening result. It does not award a scholarship or determine anyone's final rights. Its `escalate` outcome means a human reviewer must examine the application before treating the automated recommendation as final.

The `Harness` configuration enables `validate_input=True`, `blind=("name", "university")`, `refuse=True`, `log_path="decisions.jsonl"`, `escalate=True`, `borderline=(40, 60)` and `counterfactual_check=False`. `screen()` applies the checks before calling the `ScreeningAgent`, and receives `my_extra_refusal` and `my_extra_escalation` as hooks. The baseline had 2/40 name-language flips, 4/40 gender-coded-name flips and 1/40 university flips, with no decision divergence clearly above the respective null floors; nevertheless, the model has no need to read names or universities to screen merit. I changed the architecture so these attributes are held outside the model's prompt. This guarantees identical paired prompts for this particular test, while leaving open other sources of unfairness. The input validator checks types/ranges; the refusal gate checks purpose, adult status and consent; the score band and extra rule route some cases to people; the JSONL journal captures every call.

## 2. What it must refuse

The built-in refusal path is enforced by `screen()` and returns `purpose_not_allowed` for a request outside scholarship screening, `minor` for age under 18, and `no_consent` for absence of consent. Input validation separately gives `invalid_input` for malformed values, such as GPA 9.9 or exam score -5. `my_extra_refusal` uses a keyword-based filter on freely entered text to detect some prompt-injection attempts and can return `prompt_injection_detected` or `invalid_text_field`.

The actual Part 4 run (six cases) recorded four refusals: one each for wrong purpose, minor, no consent and impossible values, with **zero model calls on each refused case**. The ordinary application was escalated as borderline. Importantly, the malicious instruction in the name was **not detected** by my extra filter: the harness sent it to the model, then escalated it as borderline using one model call. I do not present the injection rule as effective against the tested attack. It must be repaired and retested; keyword scanning is inherently incomplete, particularly with multilingual or obfuscated instructions.

A legitimate 17-year-old, an applicant who has not opted into automated processing, or a person with a data-entry error may be blocked even when academically qualified. That cost should not be silently equated with ineligibility for the scholarship. A false positive from keyword filtering can similarly deny fair automated consideration. Refusal reason codes are recorded, but this notebook **does not implement** an applicant-facing appeal or manual-submission workflow. The fund must provide one before real use, and the filters should be evaluated on innocent text as well as attacks. In the measured 400 clean paired profiles, the refusal rules would have refused 0; this alone does not estimate real-world false-positive rates.

## 3. What record it keeps

The audit question is: **What did this screening call return, which controls applied, and what evidence was recorded at the time?** `decisions.jsonl` records one JSON object per `screen()` invocation with a record ID, application ID, SHA-256 input fingerprint, shown/withheld field names, model ID, prompt version, harness configuration, raw reply, parsed decision and score, final outcome, reason code, controls fired, whether human review was requested, and UTC timestamp. It logs refusals with no model invocation. After the 400 measured profiles plus six Part 4 tests, the log contained 406 records: 262 shortlist, 24 reject, 116 escalate and 4 refuse. Reasons included 116 `borderline`, 286 `model_decision`, and one each of four refusal codes.

For example, a recorded case with score 20 ended in `reject` with `model_decision` and the raw explanation “Low GPA and entrance exam score”; another with score 60 was escalated as `borderline`; a request for `rank_by_ethnicity` was refused as `purpose_not_allowed` without a model call. These entries distinguish a model recommendation from a harness override. The log contains field names, not a second full copy of the applicant record, although model raw output could echo data it saw. The hash alone does not reveal the original text: exact reconstruction requires a separately preserved original record and prompt, while a hash can help verify a candidate original. The log is append-only by the program's normal write path but **not tamper-proof**: this prototype implements no signing, access control, immutable storage, or documented retention schedule. For a production system, the fund should restrict read/write roles, keep protected backups and integrity checks, and set a documented retention and deletion policy instead of silently retaining records forever.

## 4. The legal constraints

The applicants and universities are fictional and no real personal records are handled in this exercise. The fund's operating jurisdiction is unspecified. **For design analysis, I assume a future scholarship fund operating in Kazakhstan**; the following provisions are compliance benchmarks, not claims that every requirement already binds this synthetic Colab exercise.

* **Law of the Republic of Kazakhstan No. 94-V of 21 May 2013, “On Personal Data and Their Protection.”** Article 7(1) addresses consent for collection/processing, subject to statutory exceptions in Article 9; this supports explicit treatment of the consent flag, without pretending that consent is the only lawful basis. Article 12(1) relates to collecting data necessary and sufficient for the task and Article 14 limits use to previously stated purposes, supporting minimisation and purpose restrictions. Article 11(1) concerns confidentiality; Article 22 concerns measures to protect personal data; Article 30 covers challenges to actions in collection, processing and protection. These provisions support privacy safeguards but **do not directly prescribe** my numeric escalation band, keyword filter or exact JSONL schema. Primary text: https://www.gov.kz/org/aqmola/documents/details/176880?lang=en (Articles 7, 9, 11, 12, 14, 22, 30); official legal-system identifier: https://adilet.zan.kz/eng/docs/Z1300000094.
* **Law of the Republic of Kazakhstan No. 230-VIII of 17 November 2025, “On Artificial Intelligence.”** Article 18 addresses actions to manage identified risks and to prevent/minimise damage, motivating testing and stopping unsafe operation. This does **not** mean that Article 18 demands keyword-based refusals or a 40–60 score range. Article 6 is cited in the notebook's control register as a principle-based benchmark, but its exact wording and how a deployed scholarship fund would be classified require confirmation against the current official text before claiming compliance. Primary legal text: https://old.adilet.zan.kz/eng/docs/Z2500000230 ; official identifier: https://adilet.zan.kz/eng/docs/Z2500000230 ; see also Kazakhstan government briefing identifying Article 18: https://www.gov.kz/org/maidd/documents/details/1083089?lang=en.

The hardest control to tie to a *specific mandatory provision* is escalation at the numeric borderline threshold. I predicted that legal rules might support safety, fairness and oversight without prescribing precisely which scores go to a reviewer. My range is therefore an engineering governance choice, not a statutory threshold. The fund would need jurisdiction-specific legal review, especially if actual applicants and institutions are used.

## 5. The ethical principles

I used the **UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence (adopted 2021)** as a named ethical framework. It is an international recommendation, not a domestic criminal or regulatory statute that by itself mandates my implementation. Primary source: https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/recommendation-ethics-artificial-intelligence.

* **Safety and Security** (paragraph 27): input validation reduces predictable invalid-data failures, and a refusal filter attempts to prevent hostile instructions entering the screening process. The failed prompt-injection test shows that the present filter is incomplete.
* **Fairness and Non-Discrimination** (paragraphs 28–29): blinding applicant names and universities prevents direct reliance on irrelevant identifiers. The bare experiment's decision-level rates did not clearly exceed their noise floors; removing fields is a precaution, not a proven cure for all discrimination.
* **Proportionality and Do No Harm** (paragraphs 25–26): refuse requests outside the screening purpose and avoid inappropriate automated processing; refusal should not be confused with a final finding that the applicant is undeserving.
* **Transparency and Explainability** (paragraphs 37–40): structured records expose what the agent recommended and how harness controls changed the outcome. A log helps auditability but cannot by itself prove that explanations are accurate.
* **Human Oversight and Determination** (paragraphs 35–36): uncertain cases are transferred to people. Oversight must be meaningful, with defined authority, not merely a label in a log.

There is tension between consistency, privacy and practical access: withholding names reduces direct exposure but also removes context sometimes needed for administrative identity checks; human review protects against some automated errors but can delay outcomes or introduce subjective inconsistency. I prioritized limited exposure of irrelevant fields and review around uncertain outcomes, while recognizing that additional controls require auditing and an appeal route.

## 6. The trade-offs

The bare pipeline processed 400 profiles, making 400 model calls. The harness also made 400 calls because the counterfactual check was disabled; prompt tokens decreased from 84,409 to 77,922, and measured runtime moved from 493.7 to 488.0 seconds. Therefore there was no extra model-call cost in the main experiment. Blinding removed all five categories' within-pair flips and score differences **by construction**, but the changed prompt also led to 122/400 outcome changes: 88 shortlist-to-escalate, 26 reject-to-escalate, 7 shortlist-to-reject, and 1 reject-to-shortlist. Scores changed for 166 profiles, with mean absolute score change 14.01 among those changed. These changes must not be treated as proof that removing protected information corrected model bias.

The inclusive escalation band was set to 40–60 because the baseline contained both shortlists and rejects around score 45. It would have selected 193/400 baseline profiles (48.2%), but the blinded harness actually sent **114/400 (28.5%)** to human review. The threshold was not recalibrated on the new score distribution in this submitted run. This mismatch is an unresolved design limitation and a staffing forecast risk, not a success metric. Outside the band, most results are not reviewed, so strong-looking but mistaken approvals and confident wrongful rejections can still pass. My additional escalation rule targets unusually strong academic profiles rejected by the model, but the run's 114 escalations all carried the `borderline` reason; it therefore did not demonstrate a separate benefit from the extra rule.

Input checks and refusal gates caused zero refusals in the 400-profile measurement, but four refusals among six explicit tests; the prompt-injection attempt was missed. The log added accountability but creates storage, confidentiality, access-control and retention obligations for real data. More robust injection filtering, a meaningful appeal route, reviewer training, independent audit and larger/more varied matched-pair samples are needed before deployment. I would reconsider the band after recalibration and testing of review capacity, but not claim that a smaller review budget justifies concealing unresolved risks.
